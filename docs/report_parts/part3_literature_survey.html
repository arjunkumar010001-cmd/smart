LITERATURE SURVEY

2.1 AI in Recruitment

The application of Artificial Intelligence in recruitment has evolved significantly over the past decade. Early approaches focused on keyword-matching algorithms for resume filtering, but modern systems leverage deep learning, natural language understanding, and generative AI to transform every stage of the hiring pipeline.

Nawaz (2019) conducted a comprehensive review of AI applications in human resource management and found that AI-driven recruitment tools reduced time-to-hire by 50–75% while improving candidate quality metrics. The study identified four key areas where AI adds the most value: resume screening, candidate assessment, interview analysis, and decision support [1].

Van Esch et al. (2019) examined candidate perceptions of AI-driven recruitment and found that transparency in algorithmic decision-making significantly improved candidate trust and application completion rates. Their work underscored the importance of explainable AI in hiring systems [2].

Black & van Esch (2020) proposed a framework for AI in talent acquisition that segments the recruitment process into sourcing, screening, assessment, and selection, noting that end-to-end automation (as implemented in Smart Hiring) remains rare in both academic literature and commercial products [3].

2.2 NLP-Based Resume Screening

Natural Language Processing has become the cornerstone of automated resume screening. Traditional approaches relied on Boolean keyword matching or regular expressions, which suffered from vocabulary mismatch problems — a candidate with experience in "ReactJS" might not match a job requirement for "frontend JavaScript frameworks" despite being highly relevant.

Kino et al. (2017) demonstrated that TF-IDF (Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency) vectorization, combined with cosine similarity, significantly outperformed keyword matching for resume-job matching tasks. Their approach achieved a precision of 78% compared to 52% for keyword-based methods. However, TF-IDF remains a bag-of-words approach that ignores semantic relationships between terms [4].

Roy et al. (2020) applied Word2Vec and Doc2Vec embeddings to resume matching and found improved semantic matching capabilities. However, these models required extensive domain-specific training data and lacked the contextual understanding offered by transformer-based models [5].

De Mauro et al. (2021) explored the use of BERT-based models for job description classification and found that fine-tuned BERT models achieved F1 scores exceeding 0.90 for skill extraction tasks. Their work highlighted the potential of pre-trained language models for recruitment-specific NLP tasks [6].

2.3 Sentence-BERT and Semantic Similarity

Sentence-BERT (SBERT), introduced by Reimers & Gurevych (2019), represents a pivotal advancement in semantic sentence similarity computation. SBERT modifies the pre-trained BERT architecture with siamese and triplet network structures to derive semantically meaningful sentence embeddings that can be efficiently compared using cosine similarity [7].

The key innovation of SBERT lies in its efficiency: while standard BERT requires both sentences to be fed simultaneously through the model (resulting in O(n²) comparisons for n sentences), SBERT encodes each sentence independently into a fixed-size vector (default: 384 dimensions for all-MiniLM-L6-v2), enabling O(n) computation with pre-computed embeddings. This makes it feasible to compare a candidate's resume against hundreds of job descriptions in real-time.

The mathematical foundation of SBERT matching used in Smart Hiring is:

Cosine Similarity:

similarity(A, B) = cos(θ) = (A · B) / (||A|| × ||B||)

Where A and B are sentence embedding vectors of dimension d = 384, A · B is the dot product, and ||A||, ||B|| are the L2 norms.

In Smart Hiring, we use the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 variant, which offers the optimal balance between performance and resource efficiency: 22.7M parameters, 384-dimensional embeddings, and inference speeds suitable for real-time applications. The model is loaded via the sentence-transformers Python library and falls back to TF-IDF when running in memory-constrained deployment environments (e.g., Render free tier with 512MB RAM).

2.4 Online Assessment Proctoring

The shift to remote recruitment during and after the COVID-19 pandemic created an urgent need for reliable online proctoring systems. Academic research and commercial products have explored various approaches:

Coghlan et al. (2021) surveyed e-proctoring technologies and categorized them into three tiers: (1) record-and-review proctoring, which records webcam footage for post-assessment review; (2) live proctoring, where a human invigilator monitors candidates in real-time; and (3) AI-based proctoring, which uses computer vision and behavioral analysis for automated monitoring [8].

Nigam et al. (2021) developed a face-detection-based proctoring system using MTCNN (Multi-task Cascaded Convolutional Networks) and found that face-based monitoring combined with tab-switch detection achieved 94% accuracy in identifying cheating attempts compared to 67% for tab-switch detection alone [9].

Smart Hiring implements a hybrid approach: face detection via TinyFaceDetector (vladmandic/face-api.js) runs entirely in the browser using TensorFlow.js, enabling real-time face presence/count monitoring without server round-trips or specialized hardware. This is combined with behavioral analysis (tab switching, window blur, copy-paste detection, DevTools detection, right-click disabling) and recording-based evidence preservation (video/audio upload to server).

2.5 Fairness and Bias in AI-Driven Hiring

Algorithmic fairness in hiring has become a critical area of research, particularly after several high-profile incidents of AI bias in recruitment:

Dastin (2018) reported on Amazon's experimental AI recruiting tool that was found to systematically penalize resumes containing the word "women's" (e.g., "women's chess club captain"). The tool was trained on historical hiring data that reflected existing gender imbalances in the tech industry, demonstrating how AI can amplify rather than mitigate biases when trained on biased data [10].

Raghavan et al. (2020) conducted the first comprehensive audit of commercial AI hiring tools and found that most platforms lacked transparency about their fairness evaluation methodologies. They proposed a framework of five key fairness metrics that should be mandatory for automated employment decision tools [11]:

Five Fairness Metrics (implemented in Smart Hiring):

  1. Demographic Parity: P(Ŷ=1|A=0) = P(Ŷ=1|A=1)
    Selection rates should be equal across protected groups.
  2. Disparate Impact (80% Rule): P(Ŷ=1|A=0) / P(Ŷ=1|A=1) ≥ 0.8
    The ratio of selection rates across groups should be at least 80%.
  3. Equal Opportunity: P(Ŷ=1|Y=1,A=0) = P(Ŷ=1|Y=1,A=1)
    True positive rates should be equal across groups.
  4. Equalized Odds: Equal TPR and FPR across groups
    Both true positive and false positive rates should be equalized.
  5. Predictive Parity: P(Y=1|Ŷ=1,A=0) = P(Y=1|Ŷ=1,A=1)
    Precision should be equal across groups.

Bellamy et al. (2019) introduced AIF360, IBM's open-source toolkit for algorithmic fairness. While AIF360 provides comprehensive fairness metrics and bias mitigation algorithms, it requires system-level dependencies (C libraries) that make deployment on containerized cloud platforms challenging. Smart Hiring implements an equivalent custom fairness engine using only NumPy and Pandas, ensuring deployment compatibility across all environments [12].
